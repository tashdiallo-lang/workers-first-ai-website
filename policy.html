Policy framework
Part Three of the report · pages 22–42

Five policy components, built to work together

Measurement without limits documents harm after it happens. Funding without fair-pay rules can finance a program while drivers still lose. Limits without driver authority can preserve a share of trips while pay quietly deteriorates. Each component below closes a gap the others leave open.

1. Measure 2. Fund 3. Limit 4. Protect & benefit 5. Authority Open questions
1
Policy component one

Measure how robotaxis affect drivers' work and lives

Measurement should help public officials protect drivers; it should not delay protection. Initial service limits, secured funding, and protection against loss should take effect before researchers can measure robotaxi service's effects. Later findings should guide changes to company obligations, driver benefits, and service limits.

Any law, permit, or agreement should require:

a

Robotaxi operators report their activity

Vehicles, trips, hours, miles, and fares — as group-level totals that protect individual privacy.

b

Rideshare platforms provide protected driver records

Secure data on trips, working time, earnings, and history, with limits on collection and unrelated use.

c

Independent researchers estimate the effect

Published methods and uncertainty, accounting for other market changes.

d

Officials respond to evidence of harm — or missing data

Published thresholds that trigger provisional payments, stronger protection, less service, or an end to authorization.

2
Policy component two

Use a portion of robotaxi revenue to fund driver protection and benefits

Before service begins or expands, each company should make an initial payment and provide guarantees and a reserve. We recommend holding money reserved for drivers in a legally protected fund controlled by government or an independent administrator — never by the robotaxi company itself.

Charges on trips, miles, or revenue

Recurring funds that grow with service; requires auditable definitions and company records.

Auctions for operating rights

Time-limited, nontransferable rights that raise money while enforcing a service limit.

Revenue sharing or company shares

A claim on long-term growth — requiring careful valuation, governance, and legal review.

What the model estimates (New York City illustration): a charge equal to 5% of autonomous fares raises about $32M/year at a 10% robotaxi trip share, and about $97M/year at 30% — before administration, enforcement, or reserves. These are test values, not recommended rates.

3
Policy component three

Limit expansion and preserve work for drivers

Before considering operating conditions, public officials should decide whether to authorize autonomous passenger service at all. Where limited service is permitted, the law, permit, or agreement should:

a

Set initial limits that preserve human-driven work

Covering both vehicles and passenger trips, since the same fleet can operate for more hours.

b

Review current evidence before any expansion

Publish independent findings and identify what remains uncertain.

c

Secure worker protection at the proposed service level

Confirm loss protection, benefits, administration, and reserves are funded through the next review.

d

Publish and enforce the response to harm

Missing data or inadequate funding should bar expansion outright.

4
Policy component four

Protect drivers from losses and provide a driver-shaped additional benefit

Two distinct forms of support: every driver who meets the work-history rule should receive protection against measured robotaxi-related losses — without having to prove which vehicle caused their individual loss. Second, if service is authorized, companies should finance a meaningful additional benefit that recognizes drivers' contribution to the market they built.

Options drivers and officials should evaluate:

Direct cash / fewer hours

Predictable, unrestricted payments to replace income or reduce long hours.

Voluntary retirement

Secure income and health support for long-serving drivers who choose to stop.

Health coverage / driver services

Health benefits, driver centers, legal help, financial counseling.

Revenue sharing or ownership

A claim on autonomous revenue, vehicle ownership, or company shares.

Voluntary training

Only counted as protection when wages, access, and outcomes are enforceable.

In the survey…

20 of 36 drivers picked ownership or a revenue share as most helpful; only 4 picked job training.

5
Policy component five

Set fair eligibility rules and give drivers real authority

An established group of drivers should be defined by rideshare or taxi work completed before a specified cutoff date — recognizing full-time, part-time, intermittent, taxi, and multi-platform work. Creating a new driver account should not, by itself, establish eligibility.

Who decides

Driver representatives decide the additional benefit's form, eligibility rules, and allocation — within legal safeguards they cannot waive (loss protection, human-driven-work rights, service limits).

Who administers

Qualified, paid professionals verify records, calculate payments, and manage reserves — so drivers get authority over decisions without having to run the program themselves.

What this framework can't decide alone

Two questions before service begins, five decisions only locals can make

Question one

What public benefits should robotaxi service deliver?

Robotaxis may improve independence or access for some riders while removing assistance others rely on. Officials should define the claimed benefit, the harms they'll monitor, and the evidence required to continue service — with affected riders and disability advocates helping set accessibility requirements.

Question two

Can a company meet every requirement and still turn a profit?

Our financial model estimates possible revenue from company charges — it does not estimate driver losses, benefit costs, or a company's full operating and capital costs. That question requires local analysis and verified company records before authorization.

Five local decisions this report cannot make

1. Which drivers qualify — and who remains responsible for fair pay in human-driven work?

2. How much robotaxi service will public officials allow, and what triggers less?

3. What will companies fund — and how will multiple operators share responsibility?

4. Who holds each responsibility — records, appeals, enforcement?

5. What must the public receive in exchange for authorizing service?
