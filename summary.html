Our question: can robotaxis benefit drivers? How?

Rideshare and taxi drivers built and continue to sustain today's passenger-for-hire market. We start from a clear principle: if robotaxi operations are allowed, they should improve drivers' lives rather than reduce their income or control over their time. This report offers a policy framework designed to make that possible.

We recommend that public officials require robotaxi companies to finance protection against lost driver income and a meaningful additional benefit through initial and continuing payments tied to their service. Before service begins or expands, each company should provide an initial payment, enforceable guarantees, and a reserve. Driver representatives selected under transparent safeguards should decide the additional benefit's form, eligibility rules, and allocation.

Whether robotaxi expansion will benefit drivers remains unresolved. Robotaxi companies are expanding in some markets, while workers and public officials have delayed, limited, or stopped proposals in others — from New York and Atlanta to Washington, D.C.

Our process: drivers told us their needs

We built this policy framework with input from about 100 drivers through interviews, a survey, focus groups, and unrecorded conversations. Drivers disagreed about whether robotaxis should operate, but they repeatedly raised four priorities:

1. Keep the ability to earn

They did not want robotaxis to reduce their pay or the amount of paid work available to them.

2. Recognition, not charity

Payments and benefits should recognize the work they had already performed and the value they helped create.

3. Real authority

Drivers wanted to help decide who receives benefits, which choices are available, and how driver funds are used.

4. Enforceable terms

A lesson from Proposition 22: a protection not written down and enforced is not a protection.

We estimate that 2 to 2.5 million people earn income from rideshare and taxi driving each year, including roughly 500,000 to 625,000 who drive more than 32 hours a week. Because hours alone do not show how much a person relies on driving income, eligibility rules should recognize full-time, part-time, intermittent, taxi, and multi-app work.

What research shows about the effects on drivers

Published research does not yet establish how robotaxis are affecting U.S. drivers' earnings, trips, or hours. Our review through August 3, 2026 found no rigorous published U.S. study that isolates those effects.

Gridwise, Q4 2025Trips per hour fell 5.3% in AV-active markets vs. 2.6% nationwide; hourly gross pay fell in Los Angeles, Phoenix, and San Francisco while rising nationwide. Directional, not causal.
Wuhan, 2026 studyA difference-in-differences study of 1,040 traditional taxis estimated a 10.9% short-run decline in daily income after robotaxi introduction. Shows effects can be measured — not proof for U.S. markets.
Our responseBecause the evidence remains incomplete, we recommend public officials require company records and independent analysis before robotaxi service begins or expands.

Our proposal: five policy components

The five components respond directly to the four driver priorities above, and are designed to depend on one another — each closes a gap the others leave open.

  1. 01Measure how robotaxis affect drivers — company records and independent analysis, with published thresholds that trigger a response.
  2. 02Fund driver protection and benefits — an initial payment, guarantees, and a reserve before service begins or expands, held independently.
  3. 03Limit expansion, preserve work — initial limits on vehicles and trips, with the power to reduce or stop service.
  4. 04Protect against loss, add a real benefit — loss protection for every eligible driver, plus cash, reduced hours, retirement, health coverage, revenue sharing, or ownership — decided by drivers.
  5. 05Fair eligibility, real authority — driver representatives decide the benefit's form and allocation; qualified administrators run the program; public officials enforce it.

What the financial model says

Using New York City as an illustration (about 240 million app-based trips a year, ~$27 average fare), a charge equal to 5% of autonomous fares could raise roughly $32M/year at a 10% robotaxi trip share, and about $97M/year at a 30% share — before administration, enforcement, or reserves. These are test values, not forecasts or recommended rates, and they do not show whether a company could meet every obligation and still operate profitably.

Three open questions this report can't answer alone

Legal authority

Can public officials in a particular place enact and enforce these requirements?

Financial feasibility

Can a robotaxi company meet all worker and public requirements and still operate profitably?

Driver support

Would affected drivers actually support the proposed policy for their city or state?

Because we have not answered these three questions for any particular city or state, this report does not recommend authorization in any particular place.

What comes next

We recommend funding locally led efforts in several cities where drivers and their organizations ask for support. Local drivers should choose whether to oppose robotaxi service, preserve or strengthen restrictions, or seek enforceable conditions — with shared legal, financial, and research tools so no city has to start from zero. Local workers should control the public voice; outside partners follow the goal drivers choose, not the other way around.

Other industries aren't waiting

Passenger driving is a useful first case because the technology is visible and the trips can be measured — but truck, parcel, and transit workers are already using legislation and bargaining to shape automation on their own terms:

TEAMSTERS

Advanced human-operator bills in multiple states and bargained a requirement that United Parcel Service give six months' notice — and negotiate the effects — before deploying driverless vehicles.

TRANSPORT WORKERS UNION

Its 2024 agreement with the Central Ohio Transit Authority requires union approval before autonomous vehicles enter service and bars layoffs or wage cuts caused by the new technology.

Different institutions provide different tools: cities can set conditions on permits and streets, states can regulate vehicles, public agencies can set purchasing terms, and unions can bargain over introduction, job security, and benefits. A longer-term question sits behind all of it — one idea worth further research is a public Automation Fund, separate from this driver framework, that could receive money or assets tied to automation's gains and use the returns for worker benefits, public services, or dividends. Alaska's Permanent Fund offers an analogy, not a design.

Conclusion

Workers have already changed the rules of the rideshare industry. They organized for pay, benefits, bargaining, fair treatment, and institutions that did not exist when the platforms arrived. Many drivers spend long days waiting for enough trips to cover rent, bills, and family needs. A technology that lets people choose to work fewer hours without losing income, security, or useful service could improve their lives. A technology that removes paid trips and transfers income away from drivers would make their lives worse.

Why attrition isn't enough
"The history of elevator operators shows why attrition is insufficient. Their union called jobs that disappeared when departing workers were not replaced “silent firings.” Retirement can be a genuine benefit when chosen; it cannot become the rule for allowing an occupation to disappear."
76,860 → 89,185 → 71,882 U.S. elevator operators counted in the 1940, 1950, and 1960 Census. Economist James Bessen found it the only detailed occupation in the 1950 Census whose subsequent disappearance could be attributed largely to automation.

The jobs are still here. The choices are still open. Elevator operators watched their occupation disappear. Here, drivers have helped define what a fair response must achieve while they can still shape what comes next. If we can meet that challenge, we can show we are committed to becoming whole.
